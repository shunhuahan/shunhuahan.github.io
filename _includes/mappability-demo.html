Why standard short reads don't work here

This is a live alignment simulation. Pick a duplication size, sequence identity, and read setup; the demo simulates a gene and its paralog, sequences both to 30× coverage with paired-end short reads, and aligns every pair back. Reads are colored by mapping quality. Faint orange lines mark the positions where the two copies actually differ; together with the unique sequence flanking the duplication, those are the only anchors an aligner has. The presets use the real difference map between each gene pair in the hg38 reference, and the clustering you see is the whole story: gene conversion keeps long stretches nearly identical, and those are the stretches where mapping collapses.

Simplified but honest model. Preset difference maps come from aligning each gene pair in the hg38 reference (sequences from NCBI; differences and indel events binned at 500 bp), so their clustered, non-uniform layout is the real thing, not a simulation choice; moving the size or identity slider switches to a uniform random model at your chosen values. Reads are error-free paired-end mates at fixed 30× coverage per copy, with fragment sizes jittered around the mean. A pair is anchored by any differing position either mate covers, and by any sequenced bases extending into the unique flanks of the duplication (paired-end rescue and flank rescue are both modeled; this is why the edges usually stay callable when the interior collapses). A pair covering zero distinguishing positions fits both copies equally well, so, like a real aligner, this one places it at random with MAPQ 0. Otherwise MAPQ = −10·log10 P(wrong copy), assuming a 1% chance per site that a sequencing error masks a true difference, capped at 60. "Callable" means covered by at least 8 reads with MAPQ ≥ 20, a typical variant-calling filter. Real aligners add indels and sequencing errors, but the collapse you see here is the same one that happens in real data.
